Commercial Loan Documentation

A commercial file is not a bigger residential file. It is a different document entirely. Where a house is underwritten to a borrower, an income property is underwritten to itself — its rents, its expenses, its leases, and the entity that owns it. Four documents carry most of that weight: the rent roll, the trailing-twelve operating statement, the leases, and the entity paperwork. Get them right and a commercial review moves. Get them thin and it stalls before it starts.

On residential investment property, an underwriter can lean heavily on the borrower and a single valuation. On commercial and larger multifamily property, the building has to speak for itself. The lender is buying an income stream, and every core document exists to answer one question: is that income real, durable, and worth what the deal assumes? Knowing what each document proves — and why an underwriter asks for it — is the fastest way to assemble a file that reads cleanly the first time.

Why a commercial file is different

Commercial underwriting is income underwriting. The value of the asset, the size of the loan, and the strength of the deal all flow from the property's net operating income — its rental revenue minus its operating expenses. That shifts the entire document burden away from "prove the borrower earns enough" and toward "prove the building earns what you say it does." A residential rental might close on a lease and a credit pull; a commercial or larger multifamily building will not, because the loan is being sized to the property's economics, not the sponsor's paycheck.

The practical consequence is that a commercial file is heavier and less forgiving of gaps. An unexplained vacancy, a rent roll that does not tie to the operating statement, or a lease that contradicts the stated income are not paperwork nuisances — they are underwriting questions that stop the file until they are answered. The four documents below exist precisely so those questions can be answered before they are asked. The rent roll

The rent roll is the single most important document in a commercial file, because it is the snapshot of the income the entire deal rests on. A complete rent roll lists every unit or space in the property and, for each one, the tenant, the leased square footage or unit type, the current rent, the lease start and end dates, any concessions or escalations, the deposit held, and whether the unit is occupied, vacant, or delinquent.

Underwriters read a rent roll for more than the bottom-line total. They read it for durability. A building at full occupancy with every lease expiring in the same ninety-day window carries a very different risk than the same building with staggered maturities. A rent roll where several units sit meaningfully below market invites a value-add thesis; one where rents sit above market invites a question about whether that income will survive renewal. What makes a rent roll strong is not a big number — it is a number an underwriter can trust, dated, tied to real leases, and honest about vacancy and delinquency rather than papering over them.

"A rent roll that does not tie to the operating statement is not a rounding error. It is the first thing an underwriter notices and the last thing that gets a deal declined without a conversation."

The T-12 operating statement

The T-12 — the trailing twelve-month operating statement — is the rent roll's counterpart on the expense side, and together they define the property's actual net operating income. Where the rent roll is a point-in-time snapshot of what should be collected, the T-12 is a twelve-month record of what was actually collected and actually spent: gross rental income, other income, vacancy and collection loss, and every line of operating expense — taxes, insurance, utilities, management, repairs and maintenance, and reserves.

The T-12 matters because it is the reality check on the rent roll's promise. A rent roll can show strong scheduled rent; the T-12 shows whether that rent was collected and what it costs to run the building. Underwriters use it to normalize income — stripping out one-time items, testing whether expenses are complete and realistic, and confirming that the net operating income the deal is priced on is the income the property actually produces. A T-12 with suspiciously low expenses (no management fee on a building the owner self-manages, no repair line, no reserves) is a red flag, because the underwriter will add those costs back in and re-test the deal against a lower, truer net operating income. Bringing a complete, honest T-12 keeps that recalculation from becoming a surprise late in the process.

Leases and estoppels

The rent roll summarizes the leases; the leases are the source of truth. For commercial and larger multifamily transactions, underwriters want to review the actual executed leases — or at least the material ones — because the lease is what makes the income legally real. A lease confirms the rent, the term, the renewal and escalation provisions, who pays which expenses, and what obligations bind the tenant and the landlord. Discrepancies between a lease and the rent roll are common and consequential: a rent roll showing a rent the lease does not support undermines the income the whole deal assumes.

On commercial property with significant tenants, a lender may also request tenant estoppel certificates — short documents in which each tenant confirms the key lease terms directly, including that the lease is in effect, the rent is current, and there are no undisclosed side agreements or disputes. The estoppel exists to protect the lender from a lease that looks clean on paper but has an unrecorded modification or an unresolved conflict behind it. For smaller multifamily, full estoppels are less common, but the principle holds: the income is only as strong as the leases behind it, and the leases are documents the underwriter will want to see, not just a summary of.

Entity documentation

Commercial and investment real estate is almost always held in an entity — an LLC, a limited partnership, or a corporation — and the entity documents prove who actually controls the borrower and has authority to sign. A complete entity package typically includes the articles of organization or incorporation, the operating agreement or bylaws, a certificate of good standing, the entity's tax identification number, and a resolution or authorization identifying who may execute the loan documents on the entity's behalf.

These documents are not a formality. They tell the lender who the real principals are, how ownership is divided, whether the person at the table can bind the entity, and whether the entity is in good standing in its state. Missing, expired, or inconsistent entity paperwork is one of the most common causes of a late-stage delay, because it surfaces exactly when the file moves toward closing and there is no time left to fix it cleanly. Assembling the entity package early — and confirming the good-standing certificate is current — removes a whole category of last-minute friction. The same discipline carries directly into multifamily transactions, where the entity and the property's income both sit at the center of the review.

Property-level and sponsor-level items

Around the four core documents sit the property-level and sponsor-level items that round out a commercial file. None is exotic, but each answers a question the underwriter will otherwise have to ask:

  • Purchase contract or refinance payoff. The real basis of the transaction, not an asking price.
  • Property condition and, where relevant, environmental review. Confirms the physical asset matches the income story and carries no obvious liability.
  • Insurance evidence. Proof the asset is — or will be — properly covered, at coverage the lender will accept.
  • Sponsor experience summary and real-estate schedule. The track record operating comparable assets, and the other properties the sponsor already carries.
  • Sponsor liquidity and, where required, personal financials. Confirmation the sponsor can carry the asset through ordinary surprises. Specific liquidity and net-worth expectations are set per program — confirmed per transaction.
  • Property tax bills and any service or management contracts. The fixed costs that shape the operating statement.

Assembling the file

The difference between a commercial file that moves and one that stalls is rarely the strength of the asset. It is whether the documents agree with each other. The rent roll should tie to the T-12; the leases should support the rent roll; the entity documents should match the borrower on the contract; the insurance and tax figures should reconcile with the operating statement. When those pieces are consistent, an underwriter can move through the file quickly because there is nothing to reconcile. When they conflict, every discrepancy becomes a request, and every request adds days. The broader discipline of sending one complete, consistent package the first time is laid out in how to prepare a deal submission.

The most efficient path is to assemble the four core documents first, confirm they tell one consistent story, then layer the property- and sponsor-level items around them. If you are preparing a commercial or multifamily transaction, the fastest way to know whether the file is ready is to put it in front of a real review. Frequently asked questions

What is the single most important document in a commercial file?

The rent roll, closely followed by the T-12. Together they define the property's net operating income, which drives value, loan size, and every other underwriting decision. If those two documents are complete and tie to each other, the file starts strong.

What exactly is a T-12?

A trailing twelve-month operating statement — a month-by-month record of the property's actual income and operating expenses over the past year. It is the reality check on the rent roll: the rent roll shows scheduled income, the T-12 shows what was actually collected and what it cost to run the building.

Why do the rent roll and T-12 need to agree?

Because they describe the same income from two angles. When they conflict — scheduled rent on the rent roll that the operating statement never shows being collected — the underwriter has to resolve the gap before trusting either number, and that turns into questions and delay.

Do I need actual leases, or is a rent roll enough?

A rent roll starts the conversation, but commercial and larger multifamily underwriting will want the actual executed leases, because the lease is what makes the income legally real. On property with significant tenants, tenant estoppel certificates may also be requested.

Why are entity documents such a common cause of delay?

Because they tend to be gathered last, and problems — an expired good-standing certificate, an operating agreement that does not match the signer, missing authorization — surface right at closing when there is no time to fix them cleanly. Assembling the entity package early removes that risk. See more in why transactions get delayed.

Does submitting a commercial deal guarantee funding?
