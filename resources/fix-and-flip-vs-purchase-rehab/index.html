Fix and Flip vs Purchase and Rehab Financing

Both paths fund work on investment property. They are not the same decision story. This comparison keeps ownership clean: Fix & Flip owns buy-rehab-sell; Purchase & Rehab owns the combined acquisition-plus-major-renovation capital plan. Neither page invents leverage percentages.

The real split: exit and capital plan

Fix and flip assumes the business is short-cycle value creation ending in a sale (or a refinance only if a hold plan is fully underwritten). The file is judged on purchase basis, rehab scope, ARV support, timeline, and a marketable exit. ARV and draws sit at the center.

Purchase and rehab assumes the hard problem is stacking purchase price + renovation budget + contribution + exit into one capital plan — often with heavier work, more draw discipline, and an exit that might be sale or hold. The conversation is less “what’s my flip margin?” and more “does the whole basis work under private-lender program rules?”

Many deals could be labeled either way in casual speech. Path fit depends on what is true about exit and scope — not which phrase trends online.

Side-by-side decision table

Question Leans Fix & Flip Leans Purchase & Rehab
Primary exit Sale of finished product Sale or hold/refinance after work — plan must name one primary
Work intensity Often cosmetic to moderate; still needs a real budget Often major systems/layout — budget and contingency decide the file
Capital story Purchase + rehab for a resale window One plan for basis + full renovation stack
Key tools MAO, deal analyzer, ARV guide Rehab budget, draws guide, ARV guide
Wrong use Calling it a flip when you will never list it Promising maximum leverage without contribution/ARV honesty

How the math conversation differs

On a flip, the discipline is: all-in cost vs supported finished value vs selling costs and carry — enough room that ordinary slippage does not erase the plan. On purchase-and-rehab, the same ARV logic still matters, but underwriting attention shifts harder onto whether purchase + work + soft costs can be funded under a private-lender program with realistic contribution. This site does not publish fixed “100%” or “70% of ARV” marketing rules. Those numbers, when they appear elsewhere as industry rules of thumb, are not RECR program promises.

Hard-money and private-lender vocabulary may apply to either path when short-term private capital is the market segment — without implying RECR is the direct hard-money lender.

Illustrative scenarios

Examples for decision logic only — not historical RECR transactions or quoted terms.

Scenario A — cosmetic flip

Contract on a dated SFR, paint/kitchen/bath, 60–90 day work plan, list for sale. Path conversation starts on fix-and-flip. Stress ARV with comps and run MAO before you write the offer.

Scenario B — gut renovation

Purchase price only works if systems and layout are rebuilt; exit may be sale or hold after. Path conversation starts on purchase-and-rehab with a line-item budget and draw plan. If hold is primary, also test DSCR math early.

How to choose without forcing a label

  1. Write the primary exit in one sentence.
  2. Write the work in one paragraph with a contingency thought — not a single round number.
  3. Open the matching program page and the related tools.
  4. If exit is hold after work, add bridge-to-DSCR or rental testing rather than hoping.
  5. Submit the file for path fit when the label is still ambiguous — better an honest conversation than a forced product name.
